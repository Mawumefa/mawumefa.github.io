## What Is ISO 27001, and What Are We Actually Building?

*Before we touch a single policy or risk register, let's get the fundamentals straight.*

Before this series gets into the templates, registers, and phase-by-phase build ahead, it's worth pausing on the basics. If you're new to information security, or this is your first time anywhere near an ISMS, you need a working definition of what ISO 27001 actually is before any of what follows will make sense.

## What is ISO 27001?

**ISO/IEC 27001** is the internationally recognised standard for building and running an **Information Security Management System**, or **ISMS**. It specifies the requirements for establishing, implementing, maintaining, and continually improving an ISMS, including how an organisation assesses and treats its information security risks. The current edition is **ISO 27001:2022**, and it's the version we'll be working to throughout this series.

Structurally, the standard has two distinct halves, and understanding the difference between them will save you a lot of confusion later.

The first half is **Clauses 4 through 10**. These are mandatory. There's no picking and choosing here: every certified organisation has to satisfy every one of these clauses, full stop. Think of them as the operating system for your ISMS, the requirements that tell you how to run the management system itself, not what specific security measures to implement.

The second half is **Annex A**, the catalogue of security controls. Unlike the clauses, you don't have to implement every control in Annex A. Which ones apply to you comes out of your risk assessment: you assess your risks, decide which controls are needed to treat them, compare those against Annex A to make sure nothing necessary has been missed, and record the outcome in your **Statement of Applicability**. We'll get to that document in detail once this series reaches Stage 2, but the short version is that Annex A is a menu, not a mandate.

## Clauses 4 to 10: the mandatory backbone

Here's what each clause is actually asking of you, in plain terms.

**Clause 4: Context of the organization**

This is where you understand the internal and external issues relevant to your organisation that affect what your ISMS needs to achieve, identify your interested parties, such as customers, regulators, and suppliers, and their relevant requirements, and define your ISMS scope: what's in, what's out, and why. Since the 2024 amendment, you must also consider whether climate change is a relevant issue.

**Clause 5: Leadership**

This is where top management has to demonstrate leadership and commitment to the ISMS, not just approve it. That includes making sure the ISMS has the resources it needs and achieves its intended outcomes. This clause also requires a documented information security policy and clearly assigned and communicated roles, responsibilities, and authorities.

**Clause 6: Planning**

This is where risk enters the picture properly. You define your risk assessment and risk treatment processes, set information security objectives and plan how to achieve them, and plan any changes to the ISMS in a controlled way. Everything downstream, your controls, your metrics, your audit priorities, traces back to what gets decided here.

**Clause 7: Support**

This clause covers the five things your ISMS needs to actually function: resources, competence (do the right people have the right skills), awareness (do staff understand their role in security), communication, and documented information (the policies, procedures, and records the ISMS runs on).

**Clause 8: Operation**

This is where you run the processes you planned, perform risk assessments at planned intervals and whenever significant changes occur, and implement the risk treatment plan. It's the clause that turns Clause 6's intentions into operational reality.

**Clause 9: Performance evaluation**

How do you know the ISMS is working? This clause requires monitoring, measurement, analysis, and evaluation, an internal audit programme, and management reviews, the planned checkpoints where top management reviews the evidence to make sure the ISMS remains suitable, adequate, and effective.

**Clause 10: Improvement**

ISO 27001 explicitly requires you to continually improve the suitability, adequacy, and effectiveness of your ISMS. This clause also governs how you react to nonconformities, correct them, and take corrective action to address the root cause so they don't recur.

Notice the shape of it: context and leadership set the foundation, planning and operation do the work, performance evaluation checks it, and improvement closes the loop. The current edition no longer names it, but that's the **Plan, Do, Check, Act** cycle (also known as **PDCA**) at work, and it's still a useful way to read the clauses:

| Phase | Clauses |
| :---- | :------ |
| **Plan** | Clauses 4 to 7 |
| **Do** | Clause 8 |
| **Check** | Clause 9 |
| **Act** | Clause 10 |

It repeats for as long as you hold the certificate.

## Annex A: the 93 controls, in four themes

Annex A is the standard's control catalogue, the practical safeguards, policies, and technical measures you draw from to treat the risks your assessment identifies. Each control is a reference point, not an instruction manual: it tells you *what* needs addressing, not exactly *how* to build it. Implementation guidance for each control sits in a companion standard, **ISO/IEC 27002**, and the rest comes from your own procedures and evidence. The 2022 revision reorganised Annex A into four themes, totalling 93 controls:

| Theme | Controls |
| :---- | -------: |
| A.5 Organizational | 37 |
| A.6 People | 8 |
| A.7 Physical | 14 |
| A.8 Technological | 34 |
| **Total** | **93** |

Again, you don't implement all 93 by default. You implement the ones your risk assessment says you need, and you add your own controls where Annex A doesn't cover a risk. The **Statement of Applicability** lists the necessary controls, justifies why they are included, states whether each one is implemented, and justifies excluding any Annex A control. It's one of the documents an auditor will read most closely, because it's the clearest evidence of whether your risk-based thinking is genuine or performative.

## What is an ISMS, exactly?

An **ISMS**, an Information Security Management System, is the full set of policies, controls, processes, and procedures an organisation puts in place to protect the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of the information in its care. That's the **CIA triad**: keeping data **confidential** (only accessible to those who should see it), **intact** (accurate and complete, and not altered without authorisation), and **available** (accessible when it's legitimately needed).

An ISMS isn't a single document or a single tool. It's people, technology, and process working together to prevent unauthorised access, use, disclosure, disruption, modification, or destruction of information. As the policies, risk register, and control evidence start coming together later in this series, that's the ISMS taking shape.

## Why bother getting ISO 27001 certified?

A few reasons come up consistently with clients:

**Market trust and credibility**

ISO 27001 is the world's best-known standard for information security management systems, and certification carries real weight with enterprise customers doing due diligence. It builds trust in the marketplace and tends to shorten the security questionnaires that would otherwise stall a deal.

**A stronger security posture**

When run properly, the audit cycle strengthens your security posture year after year. Internal audits and the yearly surveillance audits check that your controls still work as intended. They catch small gaps early, before they turn into incidents.

**Genuine insight, not just a checkbox**

Audits generate real insight too. Findings from an internal or external audit routinely surface gaps in a policy or procedure that nobody would have noticed otherwise. Treated well, an audit finding is a gift, not a failure.

## How certification actually happens

I find it easiest to think about the ISO 27001 journey in three phases:

1. **Implementation.** You define the scope, assess your risks, and build out the policies and controls that treat them. This is where this series will spend most of its time.
2. **Audit.** Once the ISMS has been running for a few months, you complete at least one internal audit and management review, then the external certification audit: Stage 1, the documentation and readiness review, and Stage 2, the evidence-based implementation audit. We'll unpack both later in the series.
3. **Certification & Maintenance.** Once any major nonconformities are closed and corrective action plans for minor ones are accepted, the certification body issues your certificate. It is valid for three years, with annual surveillance audits and a full recertification audit before it expires. Continual improvement is required, so you keep monitoring, auditing, and adjusting the ISMS as your risks change.

The whole process typically takes six months to a year, but you don't go promising a timeline without understanding your organisation's size, complexity, and existing maturity first. A ten-person, fully remote consultancy is a very different scoping exercise than a 500-person manufacturer with physical sites and legacy infrastructure. Committing to a timeline before you understand your scope is how you end up overpromising and underdelivering. **Scope validates timeline, never the other way round.**
